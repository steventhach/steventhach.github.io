How much to spend on Christmas gifts: the 3-step method

Every good budget answer starts from the same place: the money you will actually have, not the money you wish you had.

Step 1: Count the cash you'll have by Christmas Eve

Checking balance you can spare, plus what's left from paychecks before Dec 24, minus the bills that land in early January. That's your ceiling. A credit line isn't part of it unless you'll pay the balance off in full when the statement arrives.

The January test

LendingTree's survey of the 2025 season found 37% of consumers took on holiday debt, averaging $1,223. In November 2026, The College Investor found 38% of U.S. adults are still paying off something from last holiday season. If part of your number only works on a card you can't clear in January, the number is too big. Shrink it now, while it's a decision and not a statement.

Step 2: Take the non-gift costs out first

Food, decorations, wrapping, cards, shipping and the "we're hosting" grocery run all come from the same pot. The Conference Board's 2026 survey puts the average planned non-gift spend at $366, more than half the average gift budget. Subtract your own version before you split anything, or it will quietly eat the gift money in week three.

Step 3: Split what's left by weight, not evenly

Nobody actually spends the same on their partner and their coworker. Weights make the unequal part honest. Give each relationship a weight, add up the weights, and divide the gift fund by that total. One "weight point" becomes your unit, and every person gets their weight times the unit.

The worked example, by hand

Take a hypothetical household with $900 of holiday cash and $150 of non-gift costs. Gift fund: $900 − $150 = $750. Their list: a partner (weight 3), two kids (2 each), four parents and in-laws (1.5 each), three nieces and nephews (1 each) and one office gift exchange (0.5). Total weight: 3 + 4 + 6 + 3 + 0.5 = 16.5. One weight point: $750 ÷ 16.5 = $45.45.

How much to spend on Christmas gifts per person

There's no official per-person figure for any relationship, so here's how to set each one without guessing.

Per child

Give every child in the same tier the same cap. Kids compare, and siblings compare with forensic precision. If one child's big wish costs more, have that gift come from several people (parents plus grandparents) rather than quietly breaking the cap. Want a structure for how many gifts? Many families use want, need, wear, read; it pairs neatly with a per-child cap.

Per adult child, sibling or in-law

Agree on one number in writing, in the group chat, before Thanksgiving dinner. The awkward part of family gifting is rarely the amount; it's two people quietly assuming different amounts.

Parents

The NRF's October survey found Boomers most want gift cards (59%) and Gen X close behind (46%). That's useful: a parent cap can be met exactly with a card, so there's no "I went over because it was on sale" drift.

Nieces, nephews and grandkids

One flat cap for the whole tier. If there are a lot of them, a lower cap for everyone beats a higher cap for the ones whose parents you see most.

Coworkers and gift exchanges

Use the group's stated limit, full stop, and count tax and shipping inside it. If you're shopping for a $25 exchange, we wrote a whole guide to Secret Santa gifts under $25.

A cap is only real once gifts fit under it. That's where most budgets die: you set $68.18 for your mother-in-law, then fall in love with a robe that costs a third more at 1 a.m.

When your per-person number feels too small

Sometimes the math comes back with $14 for your brother and you feel like a villain. You're not. You're early. Fixes that keep the edge, in order of how much they help:

  1. Shrink the list, not the gifts. Adults on the list can draw names instead of everyone buying for everyone. One $40 gift beats four $10 ones.
  2. Make one big gift a group project. Three people at $30 each is a $90 gift nobody had to stretch for.
  3. Pair an experience with a small physical thing. Deloitte found half of 2026 shoppers plan to give an experience, and 79% of them want to pair it with a physical item. A handwritten "one Saturday breakfast, my treat" plus a small mug lands bigger than a rushed gadget.
  4. Raise the weight, not the total. If someone truly matters more this year, move weight from a lower tier. The total stays honest.
Rule of thumb

Never raise the total to fix one person's cap. Move weight. Totals that creep become January statements.

FAQ

How much should I spend on Christmas gifts?

Start from the cash you'll have by Christmas Eve, subtract non-gift costs like food, decor and wrapping, and split what's left by weight across the people on your list. There's no universal right number; the right one is the one you can pay without carrying a balance into January.

How much is the average American spending on Christmas gifts in 2026?

It depends on the survey. Gallup's October 2026 poll found Americans expect to spend an average of $1,011 on Christmas or other holiday gifts. The Conference Board's 2026 survey found average planned spending on gifts of $683. Deloitte's $1,654 is total household holiday spending, including travel and hosting. NRF did not publish a per-person figure for 2026.

How much should I spend on Christmas gifts per child?

Set one cap per child and keep it equal for kids in the same tier. Derive it from your gift fund with a weighted split rather than copying an online average, and pool money from several relatives if one child's big wish costs more than the cap.

How much should I spend on gifts for my parents or adult children?

Agree on a number with the family in writing before the holidays. For parents, NRF's 2026 survey found gift cards top Boomers' wish lists (59%), which makes hitting an exact cap easy.

Is it OK to spend different amounts on different people?

Yes. Weighting is normal and honest: a partner usually gets more than a coworker. Keep amounts equal within a group that compares, such as siblings or cousins.

Should I use a credit card or buy now, pay later for Christmas gifts?

Only if you'll pay it off in full on schedule. LendingTree found 37% of consumers took on holiday debt in the 2025 season, averaging $1,223, and The College Investor found 38% of U.S. adults are still paying off last season's purchases.
